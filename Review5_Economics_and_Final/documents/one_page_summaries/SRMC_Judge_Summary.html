Agrovoltaic Canopy for Protection and Sustainable Farming

Thiagarajar College of Engineering, Madurai. Six sensors tilt the panels to optimise crop light and electricity; rain is harvested. A computer simulation study (no field trial yet).

1. The idea in simple words

Solar panels are lifted 4.5 metres above the farm on steel wire. Crops grow underneath. The panels tilt to share sunlight between plants and power. In rain they close like a roof and send water into a tank for the crops. The electricity is sold to a nearby industry, so the farmer gets a monthly income.
Computer renders (Blender): the canopy, the view from under the panels, and rain mode.

2. How the panels decide the angle (5 sensors)

Sun sensor
how bright is it
Light sensor under panels
how much light reaches the crop
Temperature and humidity
is the air hot and dry
Soil moisture
is the soil dry
Rain sensor
rain means close the roof

Rule: try every angle from 15 to 60 degrees and pick the best mix of electricity and crop light (50 : 50). The light sensor under the panels corrects the model. Hot, dry air or dry soil means the crop needs less light, so the panels give a little more shade. Rain means panels go flat (3 degrees) and the water runs to the tank. One motor and one wire tilt all the panels together. In our demo the sensor values are simulated with sliders.

The six-sensor controller and how well it works

Safety and water: a faulty soil probe, rain sensor or PAR sensor is detected and replaced by a fallback; the panels may move at most 12 times an hour; the soil probe decides when irrigation runs and saves 4.8 % of the water against a timer with no water-stress days (a timer has 20).
Six sensors decide the tilt: pyranometer (sun), PAR sensor under the panels (light the crop gets), DHT11 (air temperature and humidity), soil moisture and rain sensor. Rain: flat roof to the gutter. Night: park. Otherwise it tries every angle and picks the best mix of 50 % electricity and 50 % crop light. The PAR sensor corrects the model; hot dry air or dry soil mean the crop needs less light, so the panels give more shade.

Year-long test with noisy sensors (864 cases): 99.6 % of the best possible combined score, with 82 % of the best electricity and 98 % of the best crop light value. MATLAB and the web twin make identical decisions in 40 of 40 random cases.

5. Can it stand the wind?

Designed for 140 km/h (39 m/s, the Indian code value for Madurai) with the panels held in place. Our first design with 12.5 m spacing failed, so we changed to a 5 m grid with a thicker tube, and it now passes. The weakest part, the tube, reaches its limit near 145 km/h. In a storm the anemometer stows the panels edge-on at 90 degrees (15 m/s = 54 km/h). Moving the panels in wind needs the 10 kN winch and 6 mm drive wire of the final design.

6. Is the electricity cheap enough?

Sources: Mercom India (TN industrial charge ₹7.50 per kWh, FY 2026), Indian Infrastructure and Energetica India (solar auction prices). Build cost ₹60,000 per kWp and 25 years are our assumptions.

We are not cheaper than big solar farms (₹2 to 2.6). We are close to what an industry pays the grid, and the same land also gives crops and harvested rain.

7. Honest limits

  • Everything is a computer simulation. No field or prototype measurements yet.
  • Prices (₹3 per kWh, 30 % farmer share, ₹60,000 per kWp) are assumptions.
  • Investor payback is long (about 41 years after paying the farmer share).
  • Crop yield and water are model results. Sensors in the demo are simulated.
  • The structure is a screening check, not a certified design.

Next step: build a small prototype with the five sensors and log one month of real data to compare.
