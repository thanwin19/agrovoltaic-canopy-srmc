Finite element check of the lever arm (SS316, yield 205 MPa)

Plane-stress P2 finite elements (scikit-fem), root fixed, wire force as a cosine bearing pressure on the 22 mm pin hole. The mesh was validated on a plate with a hole (stress concentration within 2 % of the textbook value) and converges for the arm; away from the fixed root the peak along the arm matches the hand calculation (92 MPa). The arm first drawn (90 mm root, 48 mm tip, 14 mm thick) is safe at the 4.1 kN design load (factor of safety 2.3) but yields before the 10.2 kN limit of the drive wire (0.9), so the arm was widened to 120/70 x 16 mm (factor of safety 4.7 at 4.1 kN and 1.9 at 10.2 kN); the 3D mechanism viewer now shows the revised arm.

Edge AI: the controller distilled into a small neural network that fits an ESP32

The full controller searches every angle, which an ESP32 cannot do. A 10 - 48 - 48 - 1 network was trained to copy the angle search from noisy readings, and is scored against the best possible angle with the same accuracy measure as the paper. The rules that need no network (storm stow, rain flat, night park) stay as plain code. The C code (srmc_nn.h) and weights were checked against the Python model on 400 test vectors.
Honest result: on single hours the network gets 98.2 % of the best possible (the full optimiser 99.3 %, the monthly plan 96.8 %, fixed 45° 92.6 %), but when it steers by itself over a whole year (closed loop, feeding back its own angle) it gets 97.3 %: better than a fixed 45° tilt (91.2 %) but below the monthly plan (98.8 %) and the full controller (99.5 %). So on an ESP32 use the full search if it fits, and the network only where it does not. It was trained at the reference site; other sites need retraining (the same weights drop to about 97.0 % on average over the seven sites, because the other sites have a similar sun and crop pattern).

Sensor attack test: the anemometer is spoofed during a storm

If an attacker (or a failed amplifier) makes the anemometer read near zero, the panels are not stowed. The defence uses a measurement the attacker does not control: the tension of the drive wire (a load cell on the winch). Wind torque pulls on the wire, so the tension gives the wind speed independently. In the 3D twin choose a spoofed anemometer in "Inject a sensor fault" and raise the wind above 15 m/s. Simulation only: tension, noise and storms are modelled (storms of 18, 24 and 32 m/s, 30 runs each); the stow is latched until the operator resets it, because the tension cannot give the wind speed once the panels are edge-on.
Result (averages over storms of 18, 24 and 32 m/s): with a spoofed zero or replayed calm and no monitor the panels stay unstowed for the whole storm (132 minutes) and the wire force is above the 10 kN winch limit for 40 minutes, all of it in the 32 m/s storms; with the monitor the attack is detected as the storm builds (about minute 241 of the day, 40 minutes before the wind reaches 15 m/s for the spoofed-zero case) and no storm minute is unprotected; a slow ramp-down is caught later (minute 283) and leaves 7.5 minutes unprotected. The price: the panels stay stowed for the rest of the day (about 330 to 350 minutes of the 720-minute day). Limits: a false storm reading (spoofed high) cannot be told apart once the panels are stowed, so it only costs electricity; it needs a load cell that the design does not yet include.
